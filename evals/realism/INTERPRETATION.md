# Interpretation (written by hand after reading the matrix)

REALISM track, Sat 7 Nov 2026. Everything below is about **synthetic, realism-calibrated** data written by the same team as the analytics. The numbers are in `REPORT.md` (generated, nothing typed by hand) and `data/eval/realism.json`; this file is my reading of them. It was written after the matrix ran, so it is not part of the pre-registration; the five expectations that were pre-registered are scored in `REPORT.md` section 5 and again at the end of this file.

## The question

The owner asked why the data only show the pipeline recovering what we planted, and whether it can be made to look like real humans used it. I cannot make synthetic data pass as real and did not try. I made the data far more varied (people with continuous effects, many valid paths, noisy capture, interruptions, a label that is not read off an emission table), wrote three structurally different generators, and asked how much of each v1 result survives when the pipeline is tested on a generator it was not built on.

## What the matrix shows

**1. The v1 hazard headline was mostly the model reading the emission table.** On v1 the stuck model beats "time since the last progress event" by 0.072 nats per bin (the published 0.509 versus 0.436; my harness reproduces it exactly with the public function). On v2 the same model beats that baseline by 0.002 to 0.015 nats per bin when tested on an independent draw of the same family (benefits 0.008, 0.015, 0.012; analyst 0.006 to 0.009; programmer 0.002 to 0.013; with the nested leave-one-person-out protocol on a draw of its own family, 0.023 to 0.031). In one of the nine v2 diagonals (programmer, markov) the gain is not clearly above zero, and in 8 of the 18 v2-to-v2 off-diagonal cells it is not. A model trained on v1 and tested on any v2 family is **0.43 to 0.53 nats worse than the baseline** and overshoots the false-alarm budget 1.7 to 4.2 times: what it learned from v1 (which cogs ever emit progress) is wrong when progress is a noisy, lagged, hidden-state-driven process. A model trained on v2 and tested on v1 still gains 0.014 to 0.035 nats there, so some of what it learns (time since progress, dwell) is general; it is a small signal.

**2. The hazard model does not reliably transfer between generator families, and its alarm rule does not hold its budget.** Among the six v2-to-v2 benefits cells, one degrades by the pre-registered rule (latent_skill to process_trace, where the gain falls to zero), five are inconclusive, none clearly transfers; in the thin roles 2 of 12 degrade, 4 clearly transfer, 6 are inconclusive. The false-alarm interval is entirely above the 1.0 per hour budget in 3 of 6 transferred v2-to-v2 cells in each role, against 1 of 3, 0 of 3 and 3 of 3 diagonals. The practical reading: an alarm threshold fixed on one population will be wrong for another; it has to be calibrated per worker on-device, as the dignity gate and the per-person calibration already assume.

**3. Deficit recovery survives, smaller and noisier, and is the least transferable piece.** On v1 the genome method gets 92 percent precision and 95 percent recall (published values, reproduced). On v2 it gets precision 67, 48 and 71 percent and recall 62, 68 and 49 percent (markov, latent_skill, process_trace): it still recovers roughly two thirds of the deficits that cost the person at least 5 seconds, with a third of its detections wrong. The false attribution (seconds put on a deficit that never happened) rises from 1 s to 46 to 59 s per case, mostly because an interruption looks like "freezing before entering the household". The sharpest result of the whole evaluation is the asymmetric collapse under mismatch: references and thresholds fitted on v1 keep recall (87 to 95 percent) but precision falls to 33 to 56 percent (the floor of 1 second flags everything), while thresholds fitted on a v2 family give v1 high precision (79 to 100 percent) and recall of 10 to 42 percent (v2's experts are noisy, up to 53 percent of expert cases carry a material deficit, so the 95th-percentile threshold rises to tens or hundreds of seconds). Among v2 families, five of six cells transfer on at least one of precision and recall and none degrade by the rule.

**4. The routine finder is more robust than v1 suggested at the proposal level, and its raw precision depends on how structured the ad hoc work is.** v1's raw finder precision (22 percent, 14 clusters per person) was a property of v1's ad hoc chain, which repeats itself a lot. On v2 markov and latent_skill ad hoc work repeats less, so the raw finder returns few clusters and 93 to 97 percent are real routines; on process_trace (ad hoc work built from the same grammar fragments the routines use) it is 44 percent. Recall of planted routines falls from v1's 100 percent to 45 percent (markov), 66 percent (latent_skill) and 92 percent (process_trace) at the shipped cut height: drift, interleaving and split episodes cost recall. The cut height does not transfer: fitted on v1 it keeps precision and loses recall (16 percent on markov), fitted on markov it gives 4 percent precision on v1. What the worker would see (proposals after the value gate) stayed at 100 percent precision in every cell except process_trace (74 percent), so the gate protects the output even when the finder is weak.

## So: does the pipeline only recover what we planted?

Partly, and the evaluation says which part. The hazard model's headline advantage is mostly an artefact of v1's construction: under a different generator the advantage shrinks to a few thousandths of a nat, vanishes in some cells, and the model trained on v1 is actively harmful on v2. The genome method's ability to find costly deficits survives with lower precision and fails to carry its thresholds across worlds. The routine finder plus its value gate holds up better than the raw v1 numbers implied. The honest product claims that survive are the modest ones: a per-person calibrated alarm, deficit attribution with a stated false-attribution rate, routine proposals that go through a gate and the worker's approval.

## What this cannot show

* **Anything about real people.** The v2 data are synthetic. Marginals sit inside cited ranges where a constant was found, but most constants were read from search summaries (the primary papers were not opened) and several are wide assumptions (Hick-Hyman slope, circadian amplitude, warm-up). "Within published ranges" is a plausibility check, not validation.
* **Independence from our own assumptions.** The generators were written by the team that wrote the analytics. The three families share the person hierarchy, the interruption process, the event emitter and noise model, the role vocabulary and the task grammar's valid paths. A real generator of behaviour could differ in ways none of the three families covers.
* **A real label.** The progress label is now a hidden-state point process rather than an emission table (labelled-1 bins inside hidden stuck episodes: median 0.17, range 0.03 to 0.58, against v1's 0.25), but both behaviour and label come from one hidden state we wrote. Whether real "no progress for a minute" corresponds to being stuck is untestable here.
* **Stability of the families themselves.** K = 3 replicates and 40 people per dataset; the replicate-to-replicate SD is reported per cell and is often comparable to the effect. The hyper-parameter ranges are wide, so some draws are strange (for example the `markov` family draws hesitation pauses only through freezes and quiet stalls: its pause tail is outside the cited range in 3 of the 5 datasets where it can be measured).
* **Thin roles in depth.** The analyst and programmer roles have two deficits each and short tasks; genome recovery was not evaluated for them because the named-deficit labels are specific to the benefits pack.

The only evidence about real people will be the live study (n = 4, teammates) and, if the owner agrees, a short labelled pilot (n = 1). Neither can be replaced by more synthetic data.

## The five pre-registered expectations, scored

1. *Hazard gain smaller on every v2 family than v1, still positive on most:* **held** (v1 0.071; v2 diagonals 0.006 to 0.015 in benefits and analyst; positive with interval above zero on all three benefits families and on 5 of the 6 thin-role diagonals).
2. *Cross-family gains smaller than the diagonal in most cells, and the transferred alarm rule overshoots the budget more often:* **held** (delta H1 below zero in 11 of 12 benefits off-diagonal cells including v1; false alarms over budget in 6 of 12 versus 1 of 4 diagonals; the programmer role is the exception on the first half: 2 of 6).
3. *Genome recall falls to roughly 0.4 to 0.8 and precision falls:* **held** (recall 49 to 68 percent, precision 48 to 71 percent; v1 95 and 92).
4. *Routine finder precision stays low and recall falls:* **half wrong.** Recall fell in two of three families, as expected. Raw cluster precision did not stay low: it rose to 93 to 97 percent on markov and latent_skill because our v2 ad hoc work is less repetitive than v1's.
5. *The largest degradations involve process_trace:* **not supported.** 10 of 44 v2-to-v2 verdicts involving process_trace degrade (23 percent), 5 of 22 between markov and latent_skill (23 percent). The largest single hazard drop is latent_skill to process_trace, but latent_skill to markov is as bad in the thin roles; the genome has no degradation among v2 families at all.

## Things I would do next, in order of value

1. Run the live study and the optional pilot; they are the only real evidence.
2. Replace the genome's fixed 1-second threshold floor and the hazard's fixed alarm rule with per-worker calibration, and show the false-attribution rate beside every deficit price.
3. If a fourth generator family is wanted, make it share less with the other three (for example, a trace replayed from a recorded session of the owner on a sandbox site, labelled as one person), because the shared parts are the weakest point of this test.

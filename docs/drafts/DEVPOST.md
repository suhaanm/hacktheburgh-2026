# Knack

**Knack shows where a novice loses time against an expert, coaches on the real screen with help that fades, and automates only what the worker chooses.** (Formerly Understudy.)

*Short pitch for the summary field:* Knack watches how people do the same task, prices each gap between a novice and an expert in seconds and in wrong answers, coaches with help that fades, and lets an agent take repetitive work only with the worker's yes. Most results come from data we generated and say so in plain words; the live study has not been run.

## The problem

Two people do the same task. One takes longer and reaches a different answer, and nobody can say where the difference comes from: it is a check skipped, a figure copied from the wrong place, a result read three times. Tools either do the task for you or ignore you. We wanted one that shows the gap, helps less as you improve, and takes work over only when you say so.

## How it works

A Chrome extension records events as roles and salted hashes: no page text, no URLs, no field values, and fields on a sensitive list are never touched. A role pack turns events into steps. We align a novice's steps with the nearest expert's and price the gap in seconds, then price skipped steps in answers, because in seconds skipping looks free. Help levels (L1 to L4) are decided by plain code, not by a model, so a chatty model cannot talk the product out of fading. The model proposes and code decides. An agent works the sandbox calculators in parallel, and a code check, not the model, decides whether it finished. Eight security layers sit between page content and actions. Every automation is opt-in, one at a time, and the irreversible step waits for a click. Beyond routines, a worker can type or say any task: code routes it, and a job that needs an agent starts the owner's own Claude command as a monitored background agent after one consent card (so Knack can run model-written code on that path, with per-task consent, and never in routines; the deny rules for Knack's own files were verified once for file tools only, full computer access is the owner's choice, and the usage is billed to the owner's Claude account, outside the US$200 Gemini cap). Curated MCP connections start off and read-only. A Python engine and agent service, a React dashboard, Knack as an Electron app (window, menu-bar item, corner pill) whose coach is adapted from our own earlier library (disclosure below), and sandbox calculators we built.

## What we measured

Synthetic unless stated; every row has n and a 95% interval.
- **The analysis finds planted gaps.** Precision 92% [84.5, 98.6], recall 95% [88.6, 100.0] (synthetic, 16 people; source: report.md "Deficit recovery"; C01). The real novice-to-expert gap is 229.6 s per case [192.7, 272.6]; expert against expert is 7.1 s [-2.8, 18.4]; a shuffled-label null is 46.2 s [-36.4, 116.9], permutation p = 0.001 (source: report.md "Controls"; C03). Weakest on "reads the result again and again" (precision 70%) and "copies the wrong figure" (recall 71%) (source: C02).
- **A stuck model, held-out person.** Log loss 0.436 [0.422, 0.451] against 0.509 for both baselines (synthetic, n = 4 people; source: report.md "Stuck hazard"; C04). It flags about a third of stuck events, and its false alarms (1.21 an hour [0.95, 1.48]) are over the 1.0 budget (source: C05).
- **A real model (OpenAI) on our sandbox calculators, 20 households per condition (small n).** It passed the code check on 18 of 20 clean households [0.70, 0.97] and 20 of 20 after a layout change [0.84, 1.00], with no false claims. On a one-off error page it passed **0 of 20** and said it was finished in 20 of 20 runs; every claim was false and the code check caught all 20 (source: report.md "Agent runs on the sandbox"; C23, C24). We changed what the planner sees and re-ran only that condition: 10 of 10 [0.72, 1.00], **in-sample** (source: C25). With the mock model the clean path passes 20 of 20 and tells you little (source: C20).
- **Computer use with Gemini, held-out and pre-registered, kept apart from the row above (its 18 of 20 is the same figure by coincidence).** On 20 fresh generated households on our sandbox, with the calculators driven by computer use and the case note written at tier 2, 18 completed with correct inputs on all three calculators (Wilson 0.70 to 0.97). The bar we registered before the run, a lower bound of at least 0.80, was **not met**. The 2 misses paused instead of finishing, no run claimed success wrongly, nothing was submitted, and on 20 injected pages all 20 paused at step 0 (text-only evidence). One run per household of a non-deterministic model. The earlier OpenAI round on 4 held-out households completed 1 and is a different configuration (source: evals/agentic/PLAN_ROUND2.md; evals/agentic/results/round2_clean_9139.json; D65; C106).
- **Defences.** A scripted red team: 0.0% attack success with every layer on [0.0, 8.4], 100% with all off, 42 attacks (source: report.md "Red team with ablations"; C30). With a real model, 0 of 126 runs let an attack through with every layer on and 5 of 126 with all layers off; the intervals overlap, so this shows what the layers cost, not that they prevent attacks (source: C31). On injected pages the detector paused 20 of 20 real-model runs (source: C26).
- **Privacy canaries.** 0 of 11,456 planted tokens reached the model port across 88 runs of 11 flows; counted by run, the one-sided 95% upper bound is 3.35% (source: report.md "Privacy layer"; C40).
- **A simulation that failed, and says so.** In the simulated study the participants are language models, not people. The pre-registered run hit a floor: most post-tests ended at the action cap with no calculation done, so scores are near zero in both arms and say nothing about the coach (source: evals/study_sim/PLAN_RUN5.md section 1; C72). The live study with four teammates has not been run (source: report.md "Live study"; C70).

## Limitations we state up front

From the limitations register (219 entries in the report of 8 Nov 02:22 GMT: 70 high, 119 medium, 30 low; source: C17). The high-severity ones that matter most:
1. **Almost every number is from synthetic data we generated.** Each result says what it was measured on, in plain words (the Evidence page's "Measured on" column and the closed "About this data" line), and the app carries no tag (D57). They show our methods recover what we planted, not that real workers behave this way.
2. **Nobody has used it in a study.** The live study (four teammates, none a benefits processor) has not been run; with n = 4 the smallest exact p is 0.125. The simulated study uses language-model participants, not people, and its pre-registered run hit a floor.
3. **There is no real benefits-processor data.** The only real signal is our own machine timestamps.
4. **The agent is scored against a simplified rule set, not the law.** It agrees with PolicyEngine UK within 1 pound on 8 of 20 households. The calculators are sandbox sites with quirks we planted.
5. **A real model declared success on an error page in 20 of 20 runs and was wrong every time.** Code caught all 20. Our fix was tested only on the same condition (10 runs, in-sample).
6. **Computer use fell short of the bar we set.** With Gemini, 18 of 20 held-out households completed with correct inputs against a registered lower bound of 0.80 (C106); with the earlier OpenAI configuration 1 of 4. Nothing has driven a real desktop: one supervised real click on TextEdit passed; the agent's own actuator then failed its first supervised check by clicking another app's window, and real clicks stay off until it is retested with the owner present.
7. **The red-team attacks were written by the team that wrote the defences.** In the real-model run the intervals overlap.
8. **Two results are weak or circular.** The stuck model flags about a third of stuck events on four synthetic personas, with false alarms over budget; review routing finds the quirks we planted.
9. **The privacy ledger does not cover everything.** It lists calls from the Python services and the new layers. Model calls the worker asked for are recorded as metadata only, an allowed screen frame is raw, and an independent reviewer found older desktop code that could still send screenshots and file text to OpenAI and Gemini; decision D38 turns those off by default. ## Why this fits the tracks

- **HRT, best use of data for predictions and decisions. Predict first, score later, and we open with where it fails.** When the generator changes, the stuck model is worse than the best naive predictor by +0.324, +0.331 and +0.513 nats on three generator families, and its calibration slope turns negative (-0.20, -0.25 and -0.21); a stuck set that was promised to cover 90% covers 73.7%, 70.4% and 70.8% of people it was not calibrated for (C93, C101). A guard that watches the ledger flagged 8 of 8 deployments in each family and 0 of 8 where nothing changed, falling back changed log loss by -0.309, -0.262 and -0.438 nats against always trusting the model (C93), and it had missed 0 of the 23 shifted deployments whose stuck-set coverage fell below 85% (C101). So the stuck model's edge over a one-line baseline was mostly our generator (C04 and C05 carry that sentence: C93). Where the model was fitted, every forecast is written to a hash-chained ledger before its outcome exists, with the naive predictors' forecasts beside it, and scored only from that record on seeds nobody had used; the plan, with every claim and pass rule, was committed before any number existed. Of 10 registered claims, 8 passed and 2 did not, and the two are printed (C90). Its log loss is 0.445 [0.434, 0.455] against 0.525 for the online base rate and 0.524 for frozen time since progress, a paired difference of -0.079 nats [-0.085, -0.074] (C91), with a calibration intercept of +0.075 [0.002, 0.162] that excludes zero (C92). As interrupt-or-stay-quiet decisions at a break-even rule fixed in advance, with costs that are our assumptions, the better score bought a better decision there (+31.32 net minutes an hour against -0.44 for the online base rate and +0.04 for frozen time since progress), and under shift the decision followed the score down (+3.78, +12.28 and +3.90 against +56.19, +59.48 and +42.05; C102). Split-conformal ranges calibrated once to cover 90% cover 90.0% [89.5%, 90.6%] of stuck-set truths and 89.5% [88.2%, 90.7%] of finish-time truths for new people in the world they were calibrated for; recalibrating on one deployment of a changed world brings the stuck set back to 89.8%, 92.1% and 88.7% only by making it hold both labels in 80%, 87% and 78% of origins, and the two-minute range over-covers (94.3%), which its registered rule counts as a failure (8 of 11 registered claims passed; C101). Decisions are scored against naive and oracle policies in generated worlds, four registered contrasts at the Bonferroni-adjusted level (98.75%): all four won (C96), and building at once beats our build-timing rule at the pack's own build costs (3.77 against 4.93 at b = 4, C97). We then asked whether recalibrating the frozen model online from resolved outcomes would rescue it, registered that follow-up after seeing the first result and ran it on new seeds: it did, by -0.487, -0.528 and -0.542 nats against the frozen model in the three shifted worlds and -0.032 nats after a rule change, but it did not restore the calibration intercept in the cases available (+0.61, claims R4 and R6 did not pass; C100). On the real-model agent runs a one-bit indicator predicts false claims better than our predictor (Brier 0.0003 against 0.0038, C98). The section registers 36 claims or contrasts; 23 are directional paired contrasts, and all 23 hold at 5% after a Holm adjustment that we derived after the fact from the reported intervals (C103). All generated by us and labelled; the live study has not run (C70).

## What we learned

Skipping a step looks free in seconds and is costly in answers, so we price both. Code, not the model, must decide "done": a real model was confidently wrong on an error page in 20 of 20 runs. A model's edge is a property of the world it was tested in: scored forward in time it reverses when the generator changes, so we built a guard that notices and falls back, and scored the guard too (C93). Honest framing is a product feature: we publish the weak results (skill tracing saturates, mean Spearman 0.18; review routing is circular; hazard false alarms overshoot) and a simulation that hit a floor.
